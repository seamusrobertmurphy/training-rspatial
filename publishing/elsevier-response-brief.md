## Task 3B: more titles

Written 3 September 2026 against the fuller series list, and using Forest Carbon
Accounting in place of Forest Greenhouse Gas Accounting, which is the better
phrase because it is what the field and the search box actually say.

### The grammar to match

Main titles take one of three shapes. "Earth Observation for X", "Earth
Observation Applications to X", or "[Method or platform] for X". Subtitles name
the mode of treatment in two or three nouns, as in Theory and Applications,
Techniques and Challenges, Algorithms and Applications, From Theory to Practical
Applications, and Technologies, Applications, Case Studies and Future Directions,
or they name a perspective, as in An Optical Remote Sensing Perspective. No
subtitle in the series names a market, a regulator or a standards body. One main
title names a platform, Google Earth Engine. None names a programming language.

That last pair of facts settles where R and where the registries can sit. R may
appear, in the subtitle, last. The registries may not appear by name anywhere,
and the way to keep the audit content in the title is the word verification used
as the third methodological stage rather than as a service.

### Ten options

Ranked. Each keeps Earth Observation first and Forest Carbon Accounting or a near
synonym as the domain.

1. **Earth Observation for Forest Carbon Accounting: Methods, Uncertainty and
   Verification in R.** Three-noun treatment subtitle in the series' own grammar,
   with verification reading as the closing stage of a method rather than as an
   audit. R last, where a tool belongs. Carries every search term a practitioner
   or a student would type.

2. **Earth Observation for Forest Carbon Accounting: IPCC Methods, Uncertainty
   and Registry Practice.** Puts the inventory frame in the title, which is the
   strongest available signal to an academic reviewer that this is measurement
   science and not consultancy. Registry Practice is the honest name for the
   audit half and reads as practice. Drops R, which is the cost.

3. **Earth Observation for Forest Carbon Inventories: Estimation, Uncertainty and
   Verification.** Inventories is the word the national monitoring readership
   uses and it inherits the whole IPCC vocabulary without spelling out the
   acronym. Slightly narrower than Accounting, which also covers project-scale
   crediting.

4. **Earth Observation for Forest Carbon Accounting: Activity Data, Emission
   Factors and Uncertainty in R.** The most precise description of what the
   second half of the book does, and the least legible to a reader outside the
   inventory community.

5. **Earth Observation for Forest Carbon Monitoring, Reporting and Verification:
   Methods, Uncertainty and Practice.** Spells out the field's own term. Long,
   and the main title carries five nouns before the colon, which no volume in the
   series does.

6. **Earth Observation for Forest Carbon Accounting: From Satellite Imagery to
   Verified Estimates.** Mirrors From Theory to Practical Applications, which is
   a subtitle the series already uses. Reads well and says less about method.

7. **Earth Observation Applications to Forest Carbon Accounting: Measurement,
   Uncertainty and Verification.** Matches the landslide volume's main-title
   shape exactly. Applications to is clumsier English than for.

8. **Forest Carbon Accounting with Earth Observation: An Uncertainty
   Perspective.** Mirrors An Optical Remote Sensing Perspective, which is the

9. **Earth Observation for Forest Carbon Accounting: Satellite, Airborne and
   Field Measurement with Uncertainty.** The only option that signals the laser
   scanning and field inventory content in the title. Wordy.

10. **Earth Observation and Forest Carbon Accounting: Bridging Remote Sensing,
    Forest Inventory and Verification.** Says out loud what the author's own
    position is, on the line between three disciplines, which is exactly the gap
    the series scope statement claims to serve. Bridging is a weak verb for a
    title and no series volume uses one.

### The two to defend

Options 1 and 2, offered as a pair with the preference on 1, and with one
sentence explaining that the difference between them is whether Petropoulos wants
the software or the inventory frame in the title, since the book contains both.

Option 1 is the better title. Methods, Uncertainty and Verification is the
book's actual structure in three words and it is the series' own subtitle
grammar. Verification sits third, where it reads as the last stage of a
measurement chain, which is both true and the framing that survives peer review.
Putting R at the end answers the discoverability instruction on the Elsevier
proposal form without leading with the software, which is the specific fault
Menka named.

Option 2 is the better title if the aim is to hold the door open for the IPCC
material, the glossaries and the guidance toolkits. Naming IPCC in a subtitle
tells a reviewer that the book teaches an internationally agreed method rather
than a set of private standards, and it makes the registry chapters legible as
national inventory practice rather than as market servicing. It is also the
version that ages best, because IPCC guidance revises on a decadal cycle and
registry documents revise annually.

Whichever is chosen, do not name Verra, ACR or ART in the title. Those names date
the book, narrow it geographically, and read to an academic reviewer as trade
literature. They belong in the chapter titles, the keywords and the index.

## Task 3C: the biometrics angle

Added 3 September 2026 after the author asked to bring the measurement
mathematics into the title.

### The word

Three candidates were floated and only two are words.

**Biometrics**, and its older synonym **biometry**, is the correct term. In
forestry it names the mathematics and statistics of tree and stand measurement,
which is exactly the content meant. It carries one serious problem. Since about
2005 the unqualified word has meant fingerprints and face recognition to almost
everyone, including to search engines and to acquisitions editors. Springer
publishes Introduction to Biometrics and the Handbook of Biometrics for Forensic
Science. Never use the bare word in a main title. Next to Forest or Forest Carbon
it disambiguates completely and is safe.

**Mensuration** is the classical term for measuring trees and stands and it is
still the word on the standard textbook. It is unambiguous, it is old-fashioned,
and to a remote-sensing series editor it will read as the discipline the book is
moving on from rather than the one it is built on.

**Biostatistics** is the wrong field. It means the statistics of health, medicine
and clinical trials. Using it here would misdirect every reader who searched on
it. Biomensuration and biometrition are not words in any field.

### What it costs

Putting biometrics or mensuration in the title changes which books the proposal's
competition table has to name, because it invites a comparison that the current
table avoids. Two titles then become unavoidable.

Mehtatalo and Lappi, Biometry for Forestry and Environmental Data: With Examples
in R, Chapman and Hall/CRC Applied Environmental Statistics, 2020, 426 pages,
ISBN 9781498711487. This is the R forest-biometrics book. It covers linear,
nonlinear, generalised linear and multivariate models with their mixed-effects
forms, plus chapters on tree size, tree taper, measurement error and forest
experiments. It is deeper on statistical theory than this book will be and it
reaches no carbon estimate, no map and no standard. Note also that it sits in the
CRC list that declined this proposal in August.

Kershaw, Ducey, Beers and Husch, Forest Mensuration, 5th edition, Wiley, December
2016, 640 pages, ISBN 9781118902035. The fifth edition specifically expanded its
coverage of biomass and carbon inventory and of remote sensing in forest
assessment, so it is closer to this book than its title suggests. It is a
classical mensuration text with no code and no accounting endpoint.

Elsevier's own catalogue already carries a book called Forest Biometrics, on
ScienceDirect at ISBN 9780080124414, which is a further reason not to use the
bare phrase as a main title.

Naming those two in the competition table is a gain rather than a cost. It shows
Menka and Petropoulos that the author knows the measurement literature as well as
the observation literature, which is the exact claim the title is trying to make.

## Task 3D: keyword titles

Added 3 September 2026 from the author's own list of ten central keywords.

### One structural point

Earth Observation and Remote Sensing are near synonyms, so a title carrying both,
one before the colon and one after, reads to a specialist as padding. There are
three clean ways round it and only one of them is bad.

The first is to swap them, putting Remote Sensing in the main title and dropping
Earth Observation. This is entirely safe in this series. Four of the sixteen
volumes lead with Remote Sensing rather than Earth Observation, namely
Hyperspectral Remote Sensing, Satellite Remote Sensing for Forest and
Environmental Monitoring, Advances in Remote Sensing for Forest Fire Management,
and Multimodal Remote Sensing Fusion and Classification. Petropoulos named the
series Earth Observation and publishes Remote Sensing titles inside it.

The second is to keep both but give Remote Sensing a counterweight in the
subtitle, so that the pair names two traditions rather than repeating one. Remote
Sensing and Forest Mensuration is not redundant. Remote Sensing and Allometry is
not redundant. Remote Sensing alone, after Earth Observation, is.

The third and best is to replace the abstract noun with the concrete modalities.
Optical, Radar and Lidar says what remote sensing means in this book, tells a
reviewer at a glance that all three are covered, and is far more informative than
the category word. The series already does this; the landslide volume's subtitle
ends "with Artificial Intelligence, Aerial and Satellite Imagery".

The bad option is Earth Observation in the main title and bare Remote Sensing in
the subtitle with nothing beside it.

### One honesty check

Big data operations is in the keyword list and is not in the manuscript. The book
is designed to render offline on a laptop, the heavy dependencies sit in
verification tracks with `eval: false`, and the scalable material amounts to two
unexecuted `sits` and `gdalcubes` sketches. A title promising big data is a claim
a reviewer will open the book to test. Either build the chapter, in which case the
claim is earned, or keep the phrase out of the title and put it in the keywords
field, where it describes intent rather than content.

A subtitle carries three to five content words. Ten will not fit and do not need
to, because the Elsevier proposal form has a keywords field whose instruction is
exactly this, to "include key terms (not already included in the title/subtitle)
that readers would use if searching for information on this topic". Split them.

## Task 2: the literature

### The R-spatial shelf

The teaching canon in R is well populated and stops in the same place every time.
Lovelace, Nowosad and Muenchow's Geocomputation with R and Pebesma and Bivand's
Spatial Data Science with Applications in R teach the methods to a standard this
book does not attempt to match and never carry a result to a reported quantity
under a rule. Baddeley, Rubak and Turner's Spatial Point Patterns is the
reference for the material in the final chapter and is a statistics monograph
rather than an applied text. Roussel and colleagues' lidR is documented in a 2020
Remote Sensing of Environment paper and an online book, neither of which is a
printed teaching text. Finley and Doser's Introduction to Forestry Data Analysis
with R, free at ifdar-book.org, covers inventory and biometrics and does not
address registries.

Against this shelf the book is not first on method and is first on the endpoint.
That claim is safe and it is also narrow, and it is the claim the CRC route was
built on. It is not the claim that will carry an Earth Observation series.

### The observation shelf

Two competitors matter far more under Elsevier than they did under CRC, and
neither appears in the current proposal's comparison table.

Esmaili's Earth Observation Using Python: A Practical Programming Guide, AGU
Special Publications volume 75, Wiley 2021, 304 pages, ISBN 9781119606888, is a
sole-authored, example-driven, code-first teaching text with exactly the shape
this book has, in a different language and without the accounting endpoint. It is
the closest structural precedent anywhere and it proves the format sells. Naming
it strengthens the proposal rather than weakening it.

Cardille, Crowley, Saah and Clinton's Cloud-Based Remote Sensing with Google
Earth Engine: Fundamentals and Applications, Springer 2023, open access, is
fifty-five chapters by more than a hundred contributors covering thirty-one
fundamentals labs and twenty-four applications. It is free, enormous, and it is
what a graduate course in applied Earth observation is most likely to adopt
today. The honest differentiation is that it teaches a proprietary cloud platform
and this book teaches an offline reproducible stack that a verifier can re-run on
a laptop with no account and no network, which is a real distinction in
verification work and should be stated as one.

The series' own Google Earth Engine and Artificial Intelligence volume sits in
the same space and is a sibling rather than a competitor, which is worth saying
to Menka because it demonstrates the series already publishes in the
tools-and-workflows register.

### The measurement literature

The book satisfies the measurement and verification literature more completely
than any competing teaching text, and the specific documents are these.

Olofsson, Foody, Herold, Stehman, Woodcock and Wulder, Good practices for
estimating area and assessing accuracy of land change, Remote Sensing of
Environment 148, 42 to 57, 2014, doi 10.1016/j.rse.2014.02.015, is the source of
the stratified area estimator the uncertainty chapter implements, and the chapter
implements it correctly including the Wi squared variance term.

GFOI's Methods and Guidance Document is now at Edition 3.0, with a 3.1 available
at reddcompass.org, and it elaborates the use of the ESA BIOMASS, JAXA ALOS-4 and
NASA/ISRO NISAR missions that came into operation across 2024 and 2025. The
manuscript cites none of this and no synthetic aperture radar appears anywhere in
the book. Under a general R-spatial series that was a choice. Under an Earth
Observation series edited by a remote-sensing specialist it is the most
conspicuous gap in the table of contents.

IPCC 2006 Guidelines and the 2019 Refinement are cited to the equation and to the
table throughout the accounting chapters, which is the standard the field expects
and which most teaching texts do not meet.

The registry documents are handled in Task 5 below, because their versions have
moved.

### Where it is first

The book is genuinely first in three places. No teaching text anywhere computes
the deduction arithmetic of three registries side by side from one uncertainty
statistic and shows the crossing point where the ordering reverses. No teaching
text demonstrates that a rare-class sampling redesign trades omission for
commission rather than reducing error. And no text I found treats the gap between
a mapped area and an area-adjusted area as a time series across a crediting
period, which is the strongest single figure in the manuscript.

It is one of several on spatial method in R, on LiDAR processing, on point
patterns and on Tier 1 emission arithmetic, and the proposal should stop claiming
novelty on any of those.

### Reviewer objections

A reviewer drawn from this series will be a remote-sensing academic who edits
collections. Five objections are predictable, and four of them are answerable.

The first and heaviest is that the book is not an Earth observation book. It
opens on projections and shapefiles, spends its first three chapters on GIS
fundamentals, and reaches its first satellite image in chapter 3 as a file read
from disk. Sensors, orbits, radiometry, atmospheric correction and the
preprocessing chain are absent. This objection is correct as the manuscript
stands and the outline revision has to answer it structurally, not rhetorically.

The second is the absence of radar. See above.

The third is that the two most Earth-observation-shaped chapters run on simulated
data. Chapter 9 classifies four synthetic reflectance bands and chapter 12 builds
a synthetic monthly index cube, and both report numbers from them. A reviewer who
opens those chapters will not accept them in this series. Task 5 sets out the
extent.

The fourth is the sole authorship, which reads in this series as thin coverage
rather than as coherence. It is answerable by naming the coherence explicitly and
by pointing at Esmaili as precedent.

The fifth is that the registry material is United States and Verra specific and
will date. That one is answerable and Task 4 handles it.

## Task 3: titles

### What the series does

Every title in the series follows one of two shapes. The first is a topic phrase
containing the words Earth Observation, then a colon, then a two-noun or
three-noun phrase naming the mode of treatment, as in Hyperspectral Remote
Sensing: Theory and Applications, Earth Observation in Urban Monitoring:
Techniques and Challenges, and Geographical Information Science: Case Studies in
Earth and Environmental Monitoring. The second is a bare topic phrase with no
subtitle at all, as in Earth Observation for Monitoring and Modeling Land Use and
Deep Learning for Earth Observation and Climate Monitoring.

No main title in the series names a programming language. Google Earth Engine
appears in one, as a platform rather than a language. The tool, where it appears
at all, sits in the subtitle. That is the convention the current title breaks, and
it is what Menka meant.

### Six options

Each is built on elements the current title does not contain. Ranked.

1. **Earth Observation for Forest Greenhouse Gas Accounting: Activity Data,
   Emission Factors and Uncertainty in R.** Matches the series' commonest syntax

   spine from chapter 8 onward and is the vocabulary the Earth-science half of
   the readership already uses. R sits last, where the series puts its tools.
   Every searchable term a reader would type is present.

2. **Earth Observation for Forest Monitoring, Reporting and Verification:
   Techniques, Uncertainty and Applications in R.** Uses the field's own phrase,
   spelled out rather than abbreviated. Monitoring, reporting and verification is
   the term the REDD+, jurisdictional and national inventory readership searches

   rather than as an audit word. The subtitle deliberately echoes Techniques and
   Challenges from the urban volume.

3. **Earth Observation and Uncertainty in Forest Carbon Estimation: Methods,

   the accounting vocabulary.

4. **Measuring Forest Carbon by Earth Observation: Land Change, Biomass and the

5. **Satellite and Airborne Observation of Forest Carbon: Estimation, Accuracy

### The two to defend

Option 1 and option 2, and they should be offered as a pair with a stated
preference for option 1.

Option 1 is the better title because it describes the book that exists. The
manuscript's second half is organised precisely as activity data times emission
factor with an uncertainty attached, and that structure is IPCC vocabulary rather

### Where it stands

Measured from the sources at commit 8d37c5f, the manuscript runs 40,204 words
across the sixteen qmd files including code, and 31,028 words outside fenced code
blocks. That second figure is prose plus tables plus inline results, and it is the
honest number to reason from. The project's own CLAUDE.md records roughly 19,000
words of prose, which no longer matches; settle which figure is being quoted
before any number goes to Elsevier.

The distribution is uneven by a factor of nearly four. Terrain and Hydrology
carries 3,234 words, Spatial Patterns 3,701, Uncertainty 3,479 and Emission
Factors 2,834, against Vector Data at 987, Getting Started at 1,162 and Raster
Data at 1,266. The three foundation chapters are lecture notes and the four late
chapters are monograph prose. That gap is visible to any reader and it is the
first thing a copy-editor will raise.

### Targets

Elsevier stated no length, so the numbers below are the author's proposal to
make, and the reasoning behind them is inference from comparable printed
textbooks rather than a publisher requirement.

A 400 to 450 page printed book with this density of code and figures carries
roughly 100,000 to 120,000 words of prose plus the listings. That means roughly
tripling the current prose. It is achievable in six months only because the
analysis is done and the argument exists; what is being written is exposition,
worked explanation and exercises, not new research.

Chapter balance should target 6,000 to 8,000 words of prose per chapter with no
chapter below 5,000. On present figures that means the three foundation chapters
need the most work by a wide margin, which is the opposite of where attention has
gone.

Figures currently number in the low tens across the book. A volume of this length
in this series would carry 90 to 120, and the proposal already says so. Almost
all should stay code-generated, because a figure regenerated by the render is a
figure that cannot contradict the text.

Equations need an apparatus they do not have. They are currently set displayed
and unnumbered. Elsevier house style numbers them within the chapter, and the
verification tracks cross-reference registry equations by number, so the book's
own equations need numbers to avoid confusion between the two. Add chapter-relative
numbering and a list of equations, which is what every registry document the book
cites does.

References number 28 entries against 35 distinct citation keys used in the text.
Seven keys resolve to nothing. A book of this length in this series would carry
250 to 400 references. That is the single largest piece of unglamorous work in the
plan and it should start immediately, because a reviewer scanning the
bibliography for their own field will notice a thin one before they notice
anything else.

### The maintenance problem

Registry documents revise faster than the book's shelf life, and Task 5 shows
this has already bitten once during drafting. The answer is structural rather
than editorial.

Move every registry constant, equation number, threshold and version string into
a single machine-readable table in the repository, read by the chapters at render
time, so that no constant is typed into prose anywhere. The verification tracks
then quote from that table and a version bump is one file edit and one render.
Publish that table as a companion resource with a dated revision log, so the
printed book states the version it was checked against and the live table carries
the current one. Say this to Menka in the reply, because a stated maintenance
mechanism is the answer to the reviewer objection that the registry material will
date, and it is the kind of companion material the proposal form explicitly asks
about under multimedia and added market value.

## Task 5: the manuscript

Read in full from `index.qmd` through `A2-automation.qmd` at commit 8d37c5f.

### Data provenance

This is the most serious defect in the manuscript and it is a factual error, not
a wording problem.

The preface states that "within-plot coordinates for the ForestGEO stems were
generated across the real footprint because the source census table omitted
them, so those maps are representative rather than a survey of record". Chapter 1
repeats it and goes further, stating that "terrain, imagery and LiDAR layers in
later chapters are simulated over the same footprint for a clean, always-runnable
progression".

Both statements were true of an earlier version of the data and are false of the
committed data. `data-raw/prepare-scbi-data.R` reads the surveyed `gx` and `gy`
from the 2008 SCBI census, applies a documented affine transform to UTM 17N, and
records in its own header that the previous version's uniform-random positions
and 2,287-stem subset were errors it fixed. `data/scbi_stems.csv` holds 38,517
stems with real coordinates. The terrain chapter runs on a real HydroSHEDS and
elevatr product for Lake Chilwa, the raster and disturbance chapters on a real
Sentinel-2 product for Rondonia, and the LiDAR chapter on the real airborne tiles
shipped with lidR.

As written, the book tells a peer reviewer that its anchor dataset is fabricated
and that its physical layers are simulated. Both callouts must be rewritten
before the manuscript goes anywhere. This is the highest-priority fix in the
document.

### Simulated chapters

Separately from the stale callouts, three chapters do build their working data by
simulation, and two of them report numbers from it.

Chapter 9, Activity Data, constructs a four-class scene over the plot footprint
with four simulated reflectance bands, stating that "forest and regrowth
deliberately overlap". From that scene it reports overall accuracy, user's and
producer's accuracy for regrowth, and a pass or fail against the VM0048
thresholds.

Chapter 12, Time Series, constructs a 108-layer monthly index cube by adding a
trend, a seasonal term and Gaussian noise, with a fire imposed in mid-2021. From
it the chapter reports a burned area in hectares and a Tier 1 carbon dioxide
emission in tonnes with a Monte Carlo interval.

Chapter 10, Baseline Modelling, simulates a seventeen-year deforestation series
and an exponential-decay risk surface. This one is defensible as written, because
the whole argument is that a short noisy series with no underlying trend can be
made to yield any baseline you like, and that argument requires knowing the truth.
It should stay simulated and should say more loudly why.

The preface sets the rule that constructed surfaces carry no reported number.
Chapters 9 and 12 break that rule. Under a general R-spatial series that was
survivable. Under this series it is the objection that decides the review.

The fix is available and cheap in both cases, because the real data are already
committed. The Rondonia product with its 2,032-point validation sample and its
per-class confusion structure is exactly the rare-class problem chapter 9 is
about, and it is already used in chapters 3 and 6. Chapter 12 needs a real time
series, which is the one thing the committed data do not contain, and acquiring a
small multi-date Sentinel-2 or Landsat stack over Rondonia and committing it is
the single highest-value data task in the plan.

### Registry citations

Checked against the primary documents in `references/standards/`. Three findings.

ACR IFM on Non-Federal U.S. Forestlands v2.1, July 2024, section 7.5. Verified
exactly. Equation 22 is Total Uncertainty and includes the harvested wood product
terms as the book says. Equation 23 is the Uncertainty Deduction and reads "if
UNCt is at or below 10 per cent then UNCDED,t equals 0, or if UNCt is greater
than 10 per cent then UNCDED,t equals UNCt minus 10 per cent". The book's function
`acr_ifm <- function(u) pmax(0, u - 10)` is correct. The stated precision
requirement of plus or minus 10 per cent at 90 per cent confidence is correct.

VMD0055 version 1.1, 21 October 2024. Verified exactly. Equations 10 and 11 give
the uncertainty discount factor for carbon stock change, zero at or below 10 per
cent and otherwise U% times t at 66.67 per cent over 100 times t at 10 per cent,
with 0.4307 and 1.6449 named as the usable values. The accuracy thresholds are
verbatim as the book states them, 70 per cent for deforestation over the
historical reference period, above 90 per cent for forest at its end, relaxing to
60 and 80 where at least half the jurisdiction's forest carries under 50 per cent
canopy cover. Note that the area inflation factor for unplanned deforestation is a
separate rule at Equation 29, which the book does not cite and does not need to.

ART-TREES. This one has moved and the book is now out of date. The constants
0.524417 and 1.645006 are confirmed unchanged, and Equation 1 remains the TREES
Crediting Level with the five-year mean and the ratchet, in both versions. But the
uncertainty adjustment factor was Equation 11 in TREES 2.0 and is now Equations 7
and 8 in TREES 3.0, split between emission reductions and removals. The book's
verification track cites Equation 11 and the project's CLAUDE.md verified-constants
table does the same. Further, the ART Board approved TREES 3.0 in June 2026 and
released it on 25 June 2026, so the version the book calls a July 2025 document is
now the operative standard, and the copy in `references/standards/ART/ART-3.0/` is
still the public comment draft. Download the approved version, recheck, and
update both the chapter and the CLAUDE.md table.

Two smaller items. The uncertainty chapter cites ACR's Framework for Remotely
Sensed Quantification of Forest Carbon as v1.0, 2026, which is correct; the
published date is 27 March 2026. The document is not in
`references/standards/`, so its two thresholds, RMSE uncertainty below 20 per cent
and 90 per cent confidence interval uncertainty at or below 10 per cent, could not
be verified against the source and should be. And the baseline chapter's note that
the Verra jurisdictional module "is issued as a draft for public consultation"
needs rechecking, because the copy in the repository is
`Verra J-ADB-UD-Jurisdictional-Activity-Data-Baseline-for-Unplanned-Deforestation-v1.0.pdf`,
whose filename carries no draft marker.

raster and activity data chapters' sits tracks show an unexecuted production
workflow, which is a tooling aside. A reader who has been told the tracks are
skippable registry material and who then finds the flowdem comparison inside one
has been misled about what is safe to skip.

Two of the three chapters with two tracks place them adjacently at the chapter
end, which reads as an appendix rather than as an interleaved aside.

The fix is to split the device. Keep the tinted verification track for registry
requirements only, and introduce a second, visually distinct box for methods and
tooling asides. The preface then describes two devices honestly and the skip rule
stays true.

Chapter 7's track also breaks the project's own rule against chapter numbers in
prose by referring to "the end of the uncertainty chapter", which is correct, but
chapter 8's two tracks refer to "the uncertainty chapter" three times in a way
that reads as a cross-reference the reader cannot follow without a link. Add
proper Quarto cross-references.

### Chapter notes

**Preface.** Structurally sound and the two-audience statement is the clearest
thing in the book. The provenance callout is wrong, as above. It says "an
appendix covers the surrounding discipline" where there are two.

**1 Getting Started.** The shortest chapter at 1,162 words and the weakest. The
provenance callout is wrong. The chapter teaches installation, projects and a
first map and does not earn its place at the front of an Earth observation book.
Under the revised outline this material belongs in an appendix or a preliminary
section, not in chapter 1.

**2 Vector Data.** 987 words, the shortest in the book. The support section is
excellent and is the one idea here no competing text explains as well. The area
check is a genuine verification workflow and the bounding box against convex hull
comparison is a real finding, correctly attributed to the 1.8 degree grid rotation.
Needs roughly six times its current length.

**3 Raster Data.** The transition from the constructed basal-area raster to the
real Rondonia product happens mid-chapter and is signposted with "everything from
here on is a real product rather than a demonstration surface", which is honest
and slightly awkward. The per-class accuracy reading is the best paragraph in the
first third of the book. The sentence "the Bolivia national land-cover work does
this at country scale" refers to material that is nowhere in the book, and the
uncertainty chapter repeats the reference. Both are dangling; either bring
Bolivia in as a case or cut the references.

**4 Terrain and Hydrology.** The strongest chapter in the manuscript and by a
distance. The measured demonstration that Lindsay's hybrid breaching bounds the
carving but not the filling, that the lake bed rises fourteen metres on average
and more than eighty at its deepest, and that the published claim about preserving
deeper basins does not survive measurement, is publishable as a short note in its
own right. The stage-area rating curve and the finding that one metre of stage
moves Chilwa's area by a factor of seven is the best applied argument in the book.
Two issues. The chapter is 3,234 words on a topic that is not obviously Earth
observation, and under this series it may need reframing around DEM products as
observational data with stated vertical error, which the chapter already argues
but does not lead with. And the Alternative implementations track is misplaced, as
above.

**5 LiDAR and Point Clouds.** Technically the most careful chapter. The
normalisation trap section, where a terrain model interpolated from an
already-normalised cloud silently returns interpolation residual, is the clearest
teaching of a silent failure anywhere in the book. One wording problem. The
dominant height section says the raster surface "sits about a metre high, the
negative bias", which is confusing because the bias as coded is mean of predicted
minus observed and the sign convention is not stated in the sentence. Rewrite so
the sign is unambiguous, and verify the reported magnitude against the executed
output.

**6 Disturbance and Risk.** The rectangularity test that fails is the most
intellectually honest passage in the manuscript and should be kept exactly as it
is. The observation that the published risk weights sum to 0.9 and that this makes
the index unauditable even though the map is unaffected is a genuine contribution.
The seasonal composite finding, that default moisture breaks on an October to
December window flagged 38 per cent of a project area against 4 per cent on a July
to September window, is the strongest empirical claim in the book and it is
uncited and unsourced. It rests on an engagement, and it needs either a citation,
a reproducible demonstration on the Rondonia data, or removal. This is also the
passage most exposed on the TÜV SÜD clearance question.

**7 Allometry and Biomass.** The five-equations-on-84-oaks demonstration, with a
4.75-fold spread from equation choice alone, is the book's best single argument
and the arithmetic checks against the reported carbon densities of 0.38 and 1.79
MgC per hectare. The biomass-weighted calibration coverage result, that the median
equation covers 61 per cent of stems but only 44 per cent of the carbon, is the
kind of finding that gets a book cited. At 1,536 words it is badly underwritten
for the quality of its content.

**8 Emission Factors.** The variance-share analysis is correct and I verified the
arithmetic independently. The quadrature total of 71.2 per cent, the 78 per cent
variance share on the growth rate, the fall to 45.8 per cent on halving it and the
70.5 per cent on eliminating area uncertainty entirely all reproduce. The
conclusion that spatial precision is nearly worthless in a project of this shape
is the most actionable paragraph in the book and it deserves to be a headline
finding rather than a subsection. The boreal sandy soil row, stated at 10 tonnes
per hectare with a 90 per cent limit resting on no profiles, is a superb teaching
example. One unsourced number, the emission factor standard deviation of 131 used
in the chapter 12 Monte Carlo, does not appear in this chapter's Table 2.5 extract
and needs a source.

**9 Activity Data.** See the simulated chapters section. The rare-class argument
is correct and important and it is being made on synthetic reflectance. The
finding that stratification trades omission for commission close to one for one,
and that only quadrupling the sample fixed it, is the sort of result that changes
practice, and it will be dismissed because a reviewer cannot tell whether it is a
property of forests or of the simulation's chosen covariance. Rebuild it on
Rondonia.

**10 Baseline Modelling.** The twelvefold spread in trend baselines across
reference windows on a series with no underlying trend is the correct
demonstration and the simulation is the right choice. The ratchet worked example
is clear. The instruction to "read the rules as anti-gaming devices and they are
carefully built" is the single best sentence about carbon standards I have seen in
a teaching text.

**11 Uncertainty.** The book's centre and it earns the position. The three-registry
comparison, the crossing point calculation, the ACR eligibility analysis showing
that above about eleven validation plots only one of the two tests does any work,
and the demonstration that eligibility is itself a random variable at fifteen
plots, are all first-rate. Three fixes. The registry equation numbers need updating
for TREES 3.0. The area-adjusted section uses a 1.96 multiplier where the registries
work at 90 per cent, which the primers appendix notes but the chapter does not. And
the eight-year mapped-against-adjusted series is hand-specified rather than derived,
so it is a constructed example reporting numbers, which is the same category of
problem as chapters 9 and 12 though far less exposed.

**12 Time Series.** See the simulated chapters section. The aliasing demonstration,
that more than a third of drifting-date draws report greening on a declining pixel,
is a real and valuable result and it is entirely a property of the simulation as
constructed. The space-time cube schematic drawn in base R is a good figure. The
Tier 1 fire chain is correct and its factors are consistent with chapter 8.

**13 Spatial Patterns.** The longest chapter at 3,701 words and the most
statistically ambitious. The standard error comparison, where the cluster-corrected
errors run six and a half to ten times the naive ones and six of seven significant
terms become one, is the most important methodological lesson in the book. The
account of the grey-stage covariate, including the value-threshold error introduced
during revision, found, and corrected, is unusually honest and should stay. The
chapter's weakness is that it is the book's only chapter with no Earth observation
content at all, and under the revised outline it needs either an observational
framing, for example detected clearings as a point pattern, which exercise 6
already gestures at, or relegation to a methods appendix.

**A1 Statistical Primers.** Good, and better placed than it is. Much of it is
prerequisite to chapters 8 and 11 and a reader meeting quadrature for the first
time in chapter 8 has already needed it.

**A2 Reproducibility and Version Controls.** Unfinished. 420 words, the shortest
file in the project. Its title is misspelled as "Repducibility" and uses an
ampersand against the project's own style. Its final section, Additional
resources, describes material that does not exist anywhere, referring to "the
workflows referenced here, standard operating procedures, demonstration
repositories with renv and version control, safeguards and QA/QC templates, and
data-service automation" without providing any of them. Its ISO 19100 paragraph
names a series rather than a standard. This appendix cannot go to peer review as
it stands.
