## Preamble

This book teaches the measurement of forest carbon from satellite and airborne
observation, and it teaches it from the beginning. That second half of the
sentence is a deliberate choice and it is worth explaining before you read
further, because it is what separates this book from most of what sits beside it
on the shelf.

The remote sensing literature is superb and it starts in the middle. Open almost
any recent volume on forest observation and the first chapter maps canopy height
from radar, or fuses Sentinel-1 with Sentinel-2 to model aboveground biomass, or
corrects the geolocation of spaceborne laser footprints. Every one of those
chapters is excellent and every one assumes you already know what a coordinate
reference system does to an area calculation, why a time series is a
three-dimensional object rather than a stack of pictures, and where the
uncertainty in a reported tonne actually comes from. The methods literature will
teach you to project a raster and never mention what a verifier does with the
result. The standards documents start on the far side of the same gap and will
tell you that an uncertainty deduction is required without ever showing you how
to compute one. In practice the two are reconciled in undocumented spreadsheets,
and that is where forest monitoring systems fail. They fail from inaccessible
workflows and thin documentation far more often than they fail from flawed
science.

So this book walks a trajectory rather than presenting a collection. The early
chapters put a shapefile and a raster in your hands, establish what a projection
does to a hectare, and settle what support means before any number is reported.
Those chapters are not remedial. The errors that end up costing a project its
credits are almost always made in the first hour of the work, in a
transformation nobody wrote down or a mask applied in the wrong order, and by the
time they surface in an audit they are buried under six months of analysis that
looks impeccable. Building the foundation slowly is the cheapest uncertainty
reduction available. Each chapter then adds one layer and closes on a defensible
number, so that by the final part a reader who began by reading a shapefile is
propagating allometric error through a Monte Carlo simulation and converting the
result into the credits a project forgoes for being imprecise.

Everything here runs. The book is written in Quarto, every figure and every
number in the text is generated by code that ships with it, and the whole thing
builds offline on a laptop with no account, no cloud credit and no network. That
is not a convenience. A verifier has to be able to re-run your work three years
later on a different machine, and an analysis that cannot be re-run has not been
documented, only described. Where a registry document is cited the citation is to
the section and the equation, so a reader can check it against the original
rather than take my word for it.

What I hope carries through all of it is the reason any of us do this work.
There is a particular pleasure in looking at a planet you have only ever stood on
and slowly acquiring the tools to map it, to measure it, and to say something
about it that holds up, and I have tried to write a book that keeps hold of that
rather than assuming the reader arrived already fluent.

## About the author

I am a forester specialising in stand mensuration and carbon verification. I
completed my PhD at SOAS, University of London in 2015 and an MSc in
Environmental Forestry at Bangor University in 2019. As a Registered
Professional Forester, number 1125, and a Forest Carbon Analyst at TÜV SÜD, I
audit carbon offset programmes under the Verified Carbon Standard, the American
Carbon Registry, the California Air Resources Board programme, the Programme for
the Endorsement of Forest Certification, and ART-TREES.

Over the years I have watched forest monitoring systems fail for reasons that had
nothing to do with the science, and that observation drives a commitment to
open-source tools and reproducible frameworks in R and Python that verification
teams can deploy and maintain themselves. From Monte Carlo uncertainty
quantification through to enhanced forest inventory protocols, the work
prioritises transparent methods, documented quality assurance and quality
control, and version-controlled workflows.

The same workflows cover applications in airborne laser scanning, synthetic
aperture radar and multispectral imagery for forest health monitoring, alongside
in-situ data scaling, positional accuracy assessment, and open-source survey
deployment systems.

## Notes on the draft

Four edits were made to the biographical block and each is reversible.

American spellings were converted to British, so specializing became
specialising and prioritize became prioritise, because the manuscript uses
British throughout.

Abbreviations were expanded. VCS, ACR, ARB, PEFC and ART became their full
names, SIFTM number 1125 became Registered Professional Forester number 1125, and
QA/QC became quality assurance and quality control. Front matter is the one place
a reader has met none of the abbreviations yet, and Elsevier's own proposal form
asks for titles and descriptions written without them.

The dense citation strings were removed from the running text. Sixteen bracketed
year links inside three sentences reads as a curriculum vitae rather than as a
preface, and a printed book cannot follow a hyperlink. Move them to a short list
of prior work under the author biography, or to the book's companion website, and
keep the prose clean.

"I have observed forest monitoring systems fail not from flawed science, but from
inaccessible workflows and poor documentation" was moved forward into the
preamble proper, where it now carries the argument, and softened in the biography
so the same point is not made twice.
